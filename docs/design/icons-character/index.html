Character + poker — five variations

Five different relationships between the player and the game, not five re-colourings of one layout.

These went through two critique passes before you saw them. First pass: the peek's cards never actually covered the face (so the gesture wasn't happening), the sunglasses read as a VR headset, and the medallion read as a life preserver. Those are fixed. The critiques below are what's still wrong after fixing — every one of these has a real flaw.
Ranked 1 of 5

B · The Poker Face

Angle: the bluff — unreadable, deadpan, shades on.

Wayfarers and a perfectly flat mouth. The joke is that the character has no expression at all, which is the one facial expression poker is actually about. Two aces sit below.

Critique. Best small-size survivor by a distance — a dark bar across a light face is a strong primitive that still reads at 40 px. But strip the cards and this is just a cool guy in sunglasses; the poker meaning is carried entirely by two white rectangles that become blobs below 87 px. The brow bar also flirts with looking like a visor.

Ranked 2 of 5

E · The Medallion

Angle: the emblem — the player as a struck coin.

Borrows the one thing that makes chip icons work — a hard circular silhouette — and puts the character inside it, with the hand fanned behind. The most "premium" of the five.

Critique. The silhouette holds beautifully to 40 px, but the cards are functionally invisible below 87 px — so for most of its life this is "a face in a coin", and the poker read rests entirely on the chip notches. The gold also pushes toward slots parlour, which is louder than the restrained dark-green table the app actually uses, and slightly at odds with a play-money app trying not to look like gambling.

Ranked 3 of 5

A · The Peek

Angle: the gesture — eyes just clearing the hand.

The single most recognisable body-language moment in poker: cards held up, everything below the eyes hidden. Raised brows do the acting. On the app's own dark felt.

Critique. Strongest story of the five, and the most brand-consistent ground. But the A/K/Q indices are wasted detail — illegible by 87 px, so I'm paying composition cost for something nobody sees. The fan is also slightly left-weighted, and with the mouth hidden and no other expression the face reads a little blank in isolation.

Ranked 4 of 5

C · The Reveal

Angle: the payoff — grinning, tabling a straight.

The emotional opposite of the peek: the moment you win. Broad smile, halo burst, a legible 10-J-Q-K-A laid out across the bottom.

Critique. The best-looking at 1024 px and the worst degradation of the set — five cards collapse into a white smear by 60 px and the halo spikes turn to noise around the head. Five cards is simply too many objects for an icon. It also over-promises: an icon showing a winning hand sets an expectation the app can't keep.

Ranked 5 of 5

D · Head to Head

Angle: the opponent — two players, one pot.

The only one that shows the social promise in the app's own name: two characters across a felt oval with the pot between them.

Critique. Most on-message, least viable. Four focal points — two heads, the pot, a card — with no hierarchy, so at 40 px it is mush. The two faces also read as a matched pair rather than as opponents, which undercuts the whole idea, and the heads crowd the top of the squircle. This concept needs one character plus an implied second, not two equal ones.

The pattern: object count predicts small-size survival almost perfectly. Everything at three or four objects survives; everything at six or seven dies. The unsolved tension is that the designs which say poker and friends best (A, D) are the ones carrying the most objects.

Where I'd go next: B's face with A's raised-brow peek framing — shades on, one card edge entering the frame instead of three. Three objects, keeps the poker gesture, keeps the 40 px legibility.
